This matrix compares the thirty-one hybrid-execution notes profiled in this section, feature by feature: two vendor API features that constrain decoding, six members that validate, mask, or repair their way to typed output (Instructor, the Atomic Agents framework built on it, Outlines, BAML, the typed language whose LLM calls are functions with the same error-correcting parser class, Guardrails, the validator-hub framework whose Guards compose installable risk validators around the call, and Marvin, Prefect's AI-functions toolkit whose four functions cast, classify, extract, and generate typed values), eighteen decision-model implementations of the no-generation contract (TypeSafe's closed Jev, the seven open answers the community shipped in Jev's launch week, CUA-S1, Jevlike, Kev, Laya, NanoJev, Nimble, and SemIf, the second-generation lines Jeff and Jeeves, Nokia's training-free AnyJev, and the board-era additions Decider, H2O-Lightning-4B, Von, Winnow, AWS's Strands Decider 2B, the architectural dissent CLM, and the full-pipeline multimodal entrant Valen), the local runtime that packages most of them (Ollaya), and four benchmarks that measure the rest of them (JevBench, the Decision Index, tool-eval-bench, the tool-calling auditor, and System1-Arena, the academic arena that hands decision models and generative LLMs identical requests across 92k questions and 138 closed-loop environments).

**These thirty are less competitors than mechanisms on one spectrum, and the decision that matters is where the schema guarantee lives, in sampling, in post-hoc checks, or in the architecture itself: I would take decoding-time enforcement everywhere it exists, treat most single-provider Instructor deployments written after 2025 as incidental complexity (and Atomic Agents as the way to keep the pattern when a whole pipeline needs it, with BAML as the language-shaped bet that the whole pattern gets absorbed into the toolchain, Guardrails as the hub-shaped bet that the checking itself becomes installable packages, and Marvin as the smallest-API bet that the loop needs no wrapping at all), and no longer take the architectural class purely on faith, because the launch week put seven independently inspectable open brackets next to Jev's closed claim, the weeks since produced two outside scoreboards for all of them plus a second generation of fork lines, a reasoning entrant, a training-free readout, a deterministic tool-calling auditor, an academic arena that scores both halves of the contract on identical requests, and a board era that has since promoted a company-built open leader (H2O-Lightning-4B), a multi-size workhorse family (Decider), the community family with the strongest third-party record (Winnow), a CPU-scale encoder (Von), AWS's eval-first entry (Strands Decider 2B, the best-published training discipline over the weakest board row), and one architectural dissent (CLM) the boards score near zero, while Ollaya packages the open side into one installable runtime.**

## Reading the matrix

**The guarantee-mechanism row is the distinction that carries the most weight, and every other row is downstream of it.**
OpenAI, Anthropic, and Outlines enforce the schema while the tokens are being sampled, so an invalid token is never drawn in the first place.
Instructor inspects the finished output and re-asks when Pydantic rejects it.
Jev, the seventeen open implementations, and the runtime that packages them sit at the end of the spectrum the other eight approach: with no text generation there is nothing to constrain and nothing to validate, which is why their rows guarantee retries and type errors away the same way the decoding-time options do.
The difference among the eighteen is verifiability, scope, and evidence, not mechanism: Jev's numbers began as vendor claims behind a closed API, and the JevBench board is the first outside reading of them (Jev led the v1.2 board at 74.4 and the initial sealed v1.4 revision at 63.3, which dropped SemIf from 73.1 to 47.7, before v1.4.2's eleven additions moved decider-4b v2, 64.1, half a point ahead and the v1.4.2.1 Plumb-4B, 65.8, and v1.4.2.2 Imajev-4B, 67.4, point releases took the top spot on consecutive days, from one runner whose methodology its own thread contests), CUA-S1 grew from a 2.8 MB MIT checkpoint anyone can re-run into a four-checkpoint family (its card now adds a 196-decision eval over actual forms and a Jev head-to-head, all still vendor-run), Laya is an Apache-2.0 multilingual family whose benchmark comparisons are likewise self-run, the launch-week wave adds a dormancy spectrum from Kev's pre-registered locked-test harness down to Jevlike's single day of commits and NanoJev's unreplicated game benchmarks, the second generation arrived as Jeff's AutoJev-fork fine-tunes, whose benchmark table matches Jev's published overall while conceding every reasoning benchmark to it, and Jeeves's reasoning-then-deciding 9B, which beats the same published numbers on its own tests at ten times the latency, while AnyJev takes the evidence lead with a peer-readable arXiv report, verified early-exit bounds, and a third-party benchmark (local-jev-bench) scoring it level with the trained engines on the public transfer sources.
The board era then filled the middle of the table: Decider fields the widest checkpoint range and the deepest (and most humbling) scoreboard history, H2O-Lightning-4B is the company-built 4B that took the open composite lead, Winnow is the family three independent readers agree on, Von puts the contract on CPUs at 395M, and CLM, the one architectural dissent, sees its contrastive-encoder argument scored at the bottom of both boards.
Ollaya packages the open side into one installable runtime whose core feature is parity-checking every model against its author's code.
Since October the scoreboard layer has doubled and the boards disagree: JevBench's open-weights main board passed Jev's unranked reference (71.5) on 2026-10-08, H2O-Lightning-4B v1.1 taking the open composite lead at 72.5 before the capability-era reshuffle, and the board at revision v1.7.47 (fetched 2026-10-24) now carries 197 ranked open-weights systems ordered by a Capability Score headline (Chon-1.0-12B 85.3 first yet only twenty-eighth by the composite, basal-omni-1.0-ultra 82.5 second, Quyet-1.0-Large 81.7 and Jeff-1.0-Large 81.5 next, the former composite leader H2O twelfth by capability and thirty-sixth by composite), while the split hosted-API board holds 44 ranked rows where kettei-fat leads the official composite and the vendor's Jev 1.14.0 row now sits unranked as a reference at 70.7 in fifteenth position (fourth by composite on the 2026-10-15 reading, second by capability), and the Decision Index 0.4.1 board (regenerated 2026-10-22, 229 configurations after the same-day regeneration) rebuilt its Full score as entirely private (60 percent same-skills, 40 percent new-domains, the public index shown but uncounted), where Fastino's shipped GLiDE V1.2 leads at 71.52 and forty systems sit above the vendor's Jev 1.14 row (60.24, forty-first) with the 1.13 reference row forty-eighth at 59.22, and the same checkpoints score tens of points apart on the two scales (Kev 27B: forty-sixth on the Decision Index, near the bottom of JevBench); JevBench v1.7.47 also announces the coming v1.7.0 protocol (landing not before 2026-10-24), which adds a 10-second limit per decision and scores over-limit decisions as failed, so the slow rows will move again, so read any board standing as a property of a protocol rather than of a model.
System1-Arena, the 2026-10-15 academic arrival, adds a fourth layer: identical requests to System-1 models and generative LLMs scored on probability quality across 92k questions plus closed-loop environments, where the largest System-1 models match fast closed LLMs off the loop at a quarter of the latency and the small ones rarely survive the loop.
In plain words: one approach makes the mistake impossible to emit, the other catches the mistake after you have paid for it, and the last two never draw a token in the first place.

**A decoding-time guarantee changes the failure mode rather than removing it.**
OpenAI staff described a confused model looping in technically valid output until `max_tokens`, and you pay for every token of it.
Anthropic refusals come back as HTTP 200 with non-schema text you must handle yourself.
The retry loop leaves your code; error handling does not.

**Given the mechanism, the library columns survive only in the niches the vendors cannot reach.**
Instructor's remaining value is the cross-provider surface (15+ providers, Ollama and vLLM included) and business rules no grammar expresses.
Atomic Agents keeps that surface but adds the piece Instructor leaves out: a component structure for pipelines where many typed calls need to compose, which is a maintainability argument rather than a guarantee upgrade.
BAML is the same class expressed one layer down: the schema lives in the language and the repair parser in its runtime, so the guarantee travels with the codebase instead of with an import, paid for in pre-1.0 toolchain churn and a provider matrix its own pivot undocumented.
Guardrails holds the hub niche: the checks themselves become installable packages (65 risk validators from PII to prompt injection to provenance), composed around any LiteLLM-reachable provider or served outside your process, with the same post-hoc failure class and a 2026 that asks who maintains the hub (hub install retired, hosted inference cut off, the company acquired by Harvey).
Marvin is the floor of the family: the same post-hoc loop exposed as four plain functions over Pydantic types, which is the right size for scripts and the wrong size for pipelines, and a library that has twice rebuilt itself (2.0, the ControlFlow merge) and published no release since March 2026.
Outlines' is regex, CFG, and recursive coverage, plus one type-driven API across local engines and hosted APIs.
The Instructor note itself argues the minimal correct design on a single vendor is native strict mode plus a thin Pydantic validation step, and I agree.

**Strict tool calls are the sharpest vendor win, because a malformed call in the middle of an agent loop is the failure retries never fixed well.**
The Anthropic note calls this the highest-value use of the feature.
Both vendors guarantee tool arguments at sampling time, Instructor can only validate and re-ask, nothing in the Outlines note grounds tool-call guarantees at all, and CUA-S1 scores form elements rather than tool calls.

**Costs follow the mechanism as well.**
The decoding-time options bill injected prompts (286 to 675 tokens per request on Anthropic) or first-request compilation (OpenAI) but nothing per failure.
Instructor pays a full model round trip per failed validation, which is the price of enforcing rules the grammar cannot express.
Outlines compiles once per schema and then runs at microseconds of overhead, at the cost of running your own stack.
CUA-S1 is free beyond your own hardware, but the general planning model above it still bills every request.

## Choosing from the matrix

- Already committed to OpenAI or Anthropic and need schema-valid JSON or tool arguments: use the native feature first and add nothing.
- Constraints are semantic (business rules, field meanings) or the stack spans providers: Instructor, layered over native strict mode where it exists.
- Serving your own models, or needing regex, CFG, or recursive structures the vendor subsets reject: Outlines as the front end.
- Running a high-throughput serving stack: prefer the engine's own backend (xgrammar in vLLM) over Outlines' engine.
- Agent loops where one malformed tool call wrecks a run: a vendor with strict tool use beats any library.
- Zero tolerance for retry latency: any decoding-time option, budgeting for Anthropic's injected tokens and OpenAI's compile latency.
- Many small decisions in latency- or cost-sensitive code paths (routing, scoring, guardrails) and no need for generated text: Jev is the hosted column priced and timed for that job, pending third-party confirmation of its claims.
- Want the strongest measured open checkpoint today and can serve vLLM on data-centre hardware: H2O-Lightning-4B, reading its open-composite lead as one protocol's verdict.
- Want one contract across laptop-to-data-centre budgets: Decider, reading its numbers per checkpoint size and per protocol, with its sealed-board collapse attached.
- Want the community family with the strongest third-party record, with text and image states, on your own GPU: Winnow, budgeting a 16 GB-class card for the 12B and accepting the private training data.
- Want a vendor-team open checkpoint with the training run disclosed seed by seed and can wait on the score: Strands Decider 2B, reading its published calibration contract as the thing to test before its 107th-place board row.
- Want typed decisions on a CPU, English-only, mid-accuracy, and accept an archived project: Von, the 395M encoder with the dual-SDK and Docker packaging, archived 2026-10-17 with weights and SDKs preserved.
- Want accuracy back without abandoning the contract: Jeeves, accepting a 3.3 s median thinking budget, self-run numbers, and an unboarded checkpoint.
- Want typed decisions from a model you already serve, without training one: AnyJev, paying rotation prefills where the stopping rule asks for them and validating calibration on your own label splits first.
- Building a multi-component Python pipeline where every call needs a typed contract: Atomic Agents over Instructor, reading it as conventions and glue, not a stronger guarantee.
- Want the least ceremony a typed LLM call can have (extract, cast, classify, generate, no wrapping): Marvin, accepting the post-hoc guarantee, the twice-rebuilt history, and a release train quiet since March 2026.
- Need risk validators (PII, prompt injection, toxicity, provenance) around LLM calls on multiple providers more than schema guarantees: Guardrails, budgeting for post-hoc validation and reading the post-acquisition maintenance signals first.
- Starting agent-native code where the typed calls should live in the language rather than an import, and pre-1.0 single-vendor churn is acceptable: BAML, budgeting for the pivot's documentation gaps on providers.
- Want the whole open wave runnable locally behind one TypeSafe-compatible API: Ollaya, reading its self-run accuracy tables as directional and its parity checks against the authors' code as the feature worth trusting.
- Want to experience the contract locally before trusting anyone's numbers: SemIf, the only column whose browser demo runs client-side with no backend, reading its explicit not-calibrated warning first.
- Shopping the wave by evidence rather than stars: Nimble hosts the only human-labeled head-to-head with Jev (76.0 versus 74.8 macro), NanoJev ships the most complete but unreplicated pipeline, and Jevlike is the dormant one-day starter whose value is historical.
- Curious whether a different architecture beats the LoRA-plus-pointer pattern: CLM is the dissent, and both boards' near-zero readings are the current answer.
- Want the systems measured rather than described: JevBench, the one column that scores every other one on frozen items with per-task artifacts, read with its thread's methodology objections and one-runner caveats attached.
- Want breadth instead of depth (42 chance-corrected benchmarks over 110,201 requests, five skill areas, private generalization tests): the Decision Index, reading its 80-percent-private Full score as one maintainer's measurement and its public suite as the re-runnable part.
- Want the same questions put to decision models and generative LLMs, with closed-loop environments on top: System1-Arena, the two-day-old academic arena, reading its missing independent reproduction first.
- About to route agent tool calls through your own serving stack and want an audit rather than a leaderboard: tool-eval-bench, seeded multi-turn scenarios with full traces, reading its author-estimated difficulty tiers as exactly that.
