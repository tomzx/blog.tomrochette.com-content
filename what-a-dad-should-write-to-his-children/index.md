Most fathers who want to pass on wisdom make the same mistake: they write advice.
"Work hard.
Be kind.
Save your money."
Their children read it once, nod, and forget it, because advice without context is a fortune cookie.
And advice given out loud is worse off still, because a conversation happens once, in one mood, at one age, and is then gone.
**A letter is different: it waits, and it can be read again at the exact moment it becomes useful, by a child who has finally grown into the question.**
That property of writing, that it survives the gap between the day you say something and the day your child is ready to hear it, is the whole reason this is an article about what to write rather than about parenting in general.

The writing that actually transmits wisdom looks different from advice, and it is older than blogging and older than parenting books.
The tradition is called an ethical will: a document that passes on not your assets but your values, and crucially the experiences that produced them.
Marcus Aurelius opened his *Meditations* the same way.
Book 1 is nothing but a list of people and what he learned from each of them.
Not maxims.
Debts.
**If you want your children to inherit how you think, the move is to write down those debts, not to issue instructions.**

Here is what to write.

## 1. Origin stories, not conclusions

Psychologists Marshall Duke and Robyn Fivush found that children who know their family's stories, how their parents met, where the family came from, what hardships were survived, score higher on measures of resilience and self-esteem than children who do not.
The mechanism seems to be what they call the *oscillating family narrative*: "we've had good times and bad times, and we got through them."

So do not write "persistence matters."
Write about the year everything went wrong, and what you did on the worst morning of it.
**The conclusion is embedded in the story, and stories are the only format children reliably retain.**

Write down:

- How you met their mother, including the parts where you almost didn't.
- The job you lost, quit, or should have quit sooner.
- The decision you agonized over, what you chose, and whether you'd choose it again.
- Where the family came from, and what it cost to get here.

## 2. Your decision-making, shown in the act

Children do not need your conclusions; they need your reasoning process, because their circumstances will differ from yours.
The most useful thing you can write is a decision journal entry from a real fork in your life: what the options were, what you knew and didn't know, what you feared, what you weighted, and how it turned out.

This is the difference between giving someone your map and teaching them to navigate.
**Your map expires; the navigation does not.**

## 3. What you got wrong

An ethical will that contains no failures is a résumé, and children can smell a résumé.
The letters that get reread are the ones with a confession in them: the friendship you let die out of pride, the money mistake, the years you worked when you should have been home.

There are two reasons to include these.
First, candor is what earns the document its authority; a father who admits error is believable when he claims certainty elsewhere.
Second, your failures are the cheapest tuition you can offer.
**They already paid for the lesson; let your kids attend for free.**

This is also the place for the harder document, the one that names the ways you were not the father you wanted to be.
You do not need to confess every shortcoming, but naming the patterns, the things you did too much of or too little of, settles a debt your children have been carrying silently on your behalf.
A written apology reaches places a spoken one, made in the heat of an argument, never quite reaches.

## 4. Specific, dated letters for milestones they haven't reached

Write letters to be opened later: at 13, at 18, at their first heartbreak, when they become parents themselves, and one for when you die.
The power of these letters is asymmetry: you're writing to a person who doesn't exist yet, and they'll be read by someone who can no longer ask follow-up questions.
That forces you to anticipate, and it gives the letter the strange gravity of a message sent across time.

Practical notes.
Date everything, because the date is part of the meaning ("he wrote this when I was three").
Resist the urge to be timeless; the dated details, what you were worried about that week, what the world looked like, are what will make the letter feel alive in thirty years.
And for the last of these letters, the one they will read when you are gone, include the practical layer alongside the emotional one: where your documents are, what you wanted done, who to ask for help.
**That letter is the one you are guaranteed they will read, and the only chance you get to choose what the last thing you say to them will be.**

## 5. The mundane operating manual

**Wisdom isn't only philosophy.**
Somewhere between the ethical will and the milestone letters, write the unglamorous stuff:

- How you think about money, not the tactics (they'll expire) but the relationship: what money is for, what you refused to trade for it.
- How to apologize properly; most adults never learn.
- What you do when you're afraid, not what you tell people you do.
- The handful of practical skills you believe in, and why you believe in them.

## 6. A record of who they were

One category of writing is not advice at all: write down who your child is right now.
The made-up words, the questions they asked at four, the thing they were obsessed with for one strange month.
Children lose their own early selves entirely, and you are the only historian they have.
**Giving an adult a detailed portrait of their three-year-old self is one of the few gifts that appreciates without limit.**
One short, dated letter per birthday is enough to build this record over the years, and an annual letter beats a perfect one.

## How to actually do it

The failure mode is waiting until you have something profound to say.
You won't, and the deadline is unknowable.
Instead:

- **Pick a cadence.** One letter per birthday is enough; an annual letter beats a perfect one.
- **Write badly on purpose.** The document's value comes from authenticity, not polish; your kids want your voice.
- **Keep a running file.** A note on your phone where observations and stories accumulate between formal letters.
- **Decide on delivery.** Some letters are given now, some at milestones, some only after you're gone; say which is which.
- **Tell them the letters exist.** Even the ones they can't read yet, because knowing they are there changes how they see you, and how you see yourself.

Write the first one this week.
Pick any category above, open a document, and put down whatever comes, without editing it into something grand.
**Treat this as a practice rather than a project, a thing you add to a little at a time over decades, the same way you would tend any other compounding asset.**
The wisdom you are worried about not being able to pass on is mostly material you have never forced yourself to say out loud, and the surprise of writing these letters is how much of it was in you all along, waiting to be asked.

## The deeper principle

The deeper principle underneath all of this is that wisdom doesn't transfer as propositions.
It transfers as narrative identity: your children absorbing your stories until your voice becomes one of the voices they think with.
You can't guarantee they'll take your advice.
But if you write the stories down, you can guarantee that decades from now, at some fork in the road you never imagined, they'll be able to ask what you would have done, and actually know the answer.

That's the whole project.
Not instructions.
**A voice they can consult when yours is gone.**
