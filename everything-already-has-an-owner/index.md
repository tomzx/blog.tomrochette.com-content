I spent part of last week unable to find anything to work on.
A few days later I was uncomfortable because a colleague might take the work I had just found.
**Those are not two problems. They are the same problem, seen from opposite ends, and the discomfort told me something my diagram could not.**

## The Diagram That Made the Work Disappear

There was no shortage of work.
The team had a large project, broken into many pieces, and at a glance it looked like there was something for everyone.
I drew a diagram to find my piece.

The diagram did the opposite of what I wanted.
Once I laid the work out and attached names to it, almost every piece already had an owner.
The few that did not looked unimportant, the kind of cleanup or polish that nobody would notice whether it shipped or not.

I had gone looking for an opening and found a closed map.
**The work existed, but the parts that mattered were spoken for, and the parts that were free did not seem worth doing.**

## "Owned" Hides "Blocked"

Then something happened that reframed the whole picture.
A colleague discovered that his own piece was blocked.
His prerequisites had not arrived, and so the work he "owned" could not actually move.

This is the flaw the diagram was hiding.
An owned piece and a moving piece look identical on a map of names and arrows.
**Ownership is not the same as progress, and a claim that cannot proceed is barely a claim at all.**

My diagram had counted his work as allocated, which made the system look fuller than it was.
The work I thought had no opening was, in part, work that was stuck, and stuck work does not stay stuck in place.
It ejects its owner back into the pool of people looking for something to do.

## The Blocked Colleague Becomes a Free Agent

That is exactly what happened.
With his real work blocked, my colleague had to find something else, and fast.
**He started doing what I had been doing: looking around for an unclaimed piece, or a claimed piece he could join.**

So he came and asked me what I was working on.

## Why the Question Felt Like a Threat

I should have been glad to be asked.
The team had a person with capacity, and I had context on a piece of work.
This is the most ordinary coordination conversation a team can have.

Instead, I felt a flash of protectiveness.
I had spent days looking for this piece, and now someone was circling it, and the first thought in my head was that I was about to lose something.

The confusing part is that my claim was not weak.
I had aligned with the team lead on this piece.
It was mine by agreement, not just by momentum, so there was no real danger of it being taken from me.

So what was I afraid of losing?
Not the work.
The solitude of it.
**A colleague with capacity is rarely a threat to your claim; he is a threat to your autonomy on the piece.**
The team lead's backing settled what I would work on, but never whether I would work on it alone.
And the unstated default on the team is that idle people join existing work rather than hunt for their own, which meant his question, however innocent, pointed at a boundary nobody had drawn.

## Finding Work and Losing Work Are the Same Scarcity

This is where the two halves connect, and the scarce thing turns out to be subtler than "unowned work."

What was scarce was a piece I could do by myself.
Finding one had been hard, because most interesting work already had people near it.
Holding onto its solo nature felt fragile, because the moment a colleague went idle, the default on the team pushed him toward existing work rather than toward finding his own.

**The scarcity that made the search hard is the scarcity that made the solitude feel fragile: a piece you get to own end to end is rare, and rare things get shared away easily.**
And both anxieties point at the same gap.
The team had a way to assign work, through the team lead, but no rule for what happens when someone becomes free mid-stream.
So the question of whether idle people join in or find their own got settled by social friction, which is exactly the friction I felt when he asked.

## What I Take From This

The discomfort is data, not a flaw in my character.
It tells me that the team had a way to claim work but no rule for the boundaries around it, and that undefined boundaries get policed by social friction exactly like the friction I felt.

A few things follow.

Surface blocked work as unallocated.
My colleague's blocked piece still counted as his on the map, which is why the map looked full while the team still had idle capacity.
A piece that cannot move should be marked as stuck, not as owned, so the person freed up can be matched to real work instead of hunting for it in private.

Name the kind of ownership you want.
My claim to the work was already stated, to the team lead.
What I never stated was that I wanted to do it alone.
Saying "I want to take this piece by myself" turns an unstated preference into an explicit one, and an explicit preference can be discussed instead of defended in silence.

Treat the question as a signal, not an attack.
When a colleague asks what you are working on, it usually means they need work, not that they want yours.
The move is to help them find their own piece, or to genuinely decide whether this one should be shared, not to tighten my grip and hope they go away.

**The work you fight to find is the work you will guard hardest, and what you are guarding is rarely the task. It is the chance to do a piece end to end on your own, which is scarcer than the work itself.**
